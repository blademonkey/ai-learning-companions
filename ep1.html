Companion 1: What AI actually is

Companion to episode 1 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

A language model predicts likely text. That makes it fluent and useful, and it also means it can state wrong things in a confident tone. It works only from what is on its "desk" (the text in front of it). So the practical skill is not finding a smarter model. It is putting the right material on the desk and checking the one claim that matters.

Key terms

Term Plain meaning
AI A label for many methods, coined in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal. The field has cycled between big promises and sober reality.
Language model A system trained to predict the next piece of text. It produces answers by repeating that step.
Desk Everything the model can see right now: your question, attached files, search results, hidden product instructions.
Knowledge cutoff The date training stopped. Later events are unknown unless the product supplies them as text.
Hallucination Fluent, specific, wrong output. Surveyed in the research literature.
Predictive, generative, agentic AI Predicts a number or label; produces new content; takes actions toward a goal. Each needs a different question: how accurate, how checkable, how reversible.

Head-to-head: three approaches to building AI

Rules written by people Learning from examples Deep learning (large models)
Strength Readable, predictable Handles messy data Handles language, images and more at scale
Weakness Brittle outside the rules Needs good examples Hard to inspect; can be confidently wrong
Ask Who wrote the rules and when? What examples, and are they like mine? What did you check, and what is my fallback?

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A team lead asks a chatbot, "What is our refund policy for annual plans?" with nothing attached. The answer sounds precise. It is a pattern of typical refund policies, not theirs. She pastes the actual policy and asks it to answer only from that text, quote the sentence used, and say so if the answer is missing. The second answer is shorter, quotable and checkable. She checks the one number a customer will rely on.

Generalist decision card

  • Before relying on an answer, ask: is the model working from my material or from general patterns?
  • Let AI draft freely. Let it act only where mistakes are cheap and reversible. Require a human yes before anything that sends, spends, deletes or speaks for you.
  • Do not share confidential documents with an AI tool without checking your employer's rules and the tool's data terms.

Counterpoints and limits

  • "Prediction" is a simple description of a complex system. Researchers debate how much understanding, if any, sits behind the fluency. The episode keeps to what is observable.
  • Agreement between two answers is not correctness. A model can repeat the same mistake.
  • The three-way split (predictive, generative, agentic) is a useful sorting, not a standard taxonomy.

Try it (10 minutes)

  1. Ask about a small, specific, non-famous detail. Note how confident the tone is.
  2. Ask the same open question twice in separate chats. Note what differs.
  3. Ask a question about a non-sensitive document, once without it and once attached with "answer only from this document."

Quiz (answers below)

  1. Why can a fluent answer still be wrong?
  2. What is the "desk"?
  3. When a chatbot seems to remember you, what is usually happening?
  4. Name one action AI should not take without a human yes.
  5. Where on a maturity ladder is "ask a chatbot and hope"?
Show answers

1. It is trained to produce likely text, not checked facts. 2. The text the model can see right now. 3. The product stores notes and places them on the desk; the model itself starts fresh. 4. Sending, spending, deleting or speaking for you. 5. The improvised rung.

Maturity thread

Improvised use (ask and accept) to repeatable use (a standard way to supply material and check output). The next rung is a method, not a bigger model.
