Two research assistants

Same 200 articles, same hard question that needs ideas from six of them.

Assistant 1: perfect indexFor every question, run to the shelves, pull relevant pieces, read them fresh, answer. Tomorrow, start over.
Assistant 2: read everything onceBuilt notes: a page per idea, person and source, with links and a running list of where sources disagree. Updates them as articles arrive. Answers from the notes, then checks originals for the key points.

Assistant 2 is the idea behind an "LLM wiki": a knowledge base an AI writes and maintains, so the synthesis is done once and kept current. The second of the knowledge approaches, after RAG.

Karpathy's "LLM Wiki" idea and its three layers

Described as what a public note says, not an endorsement as proven. Normal document use: upload files, the model retrieves chunks at question time, nothing accumulates. His alternative: the model builds and maintains a persistent, interlinked wiki of markdown files between you and the raw sources. New source: read, extract, integrate, update pages, note contradictions. Compiled once, kept current.

1. Raw sourcesArticles, papers, data. Immutable: the model reads, never changes them. The source of truth.
2. The wikiModel-written markdown pages: summaries, people and concepts, comparisons, overviews. The model owns and keeps it consistent.
3. The schemaA document telling the model how the wiki is structured, the conventions and the steps to follow.

Three operations and two helper files

IngestAdd a source; the model reads it, writes a summary page, updates related pages, logs what it did.
QueryAsk; the model reads relevant pages and answers with citations. Good answers can be filed back as pages.
LintPeriodic health check for contradictions, stale claims, orphan pages, missing links.
Index fileLists every page with a one-line summary; the model reads it first.
Log fileAppend-only record of what happened and when.

His claim from experience: the index works surprisingly well at moderate scale (about a hundred sources, hundreds of pages) without embedding-based retrieval. It also implies a limit: much larger scale may need more.

Connecting to episode 3

SECI: combinationAn LLM wiki is automated combination with a human steering.
Five knowledge problemsLint attacks conflicting and stale. Index and log address scattered.
OwnershipThe schema and the human curator address unowned.

The evidence: demonstration, practitioner report, evaluated result

A practitioner tutorialA creator teaching Obsidian and AI workflows (a July 2026 post): wiki as world knowledge, agent memory as instructions to the librarian; and controlling what an agent can see and change. Based on his own setup.
A company describing its own designPerplexity (August 2026 post): "Brain", a structured wiki of markdown files for agent memory, compiled offline, citations to sources. A vendor describing its own system; read its validation section directly.
Simpler versionsA widely viewed public article on a flat-file "second brain" built with an index.

What is missing

Independent controlled comparisonsNo rigorous head-to-head test found showing a compiled wiki beats retrieval on answer quality across typical tasks. It may well work. The honest label: promising and unproven.

Four approaches, five dimensions (part 1)

Dimensions: setup effort, whole-collection questions, traceability, freshness, risk.

Long context (paste everything)Setup: almost none. Whole-collection: possible if it fits and is used well. Freshness: you paste the latest. Risk: cost grows; middle of long inputs used less reliably. Best for small material, one-off questions.
RAGSetup: moderate to high to do well. Whole-collection: weak. Traceability: strong when built to cite. Freshness: re-index. Risk: retrieval misses, context-less chunks. Best for large collections and lookups.
Compiled wikiSetup: moderate (schema, workflow, review). Whole-collection: potentially strong. Traceability: strong only if you enforce links. Freshness: needs ingest and lint. Risk: wrong summaries compound. Best for a focused, evolving body of material.

The simple second brain, and no clear winner

Simple second brain: flat files with an indexSetup: very low. Whole-collection: limited. Traceability: simple, one topic per file. Freshness: entirely on the human. Risk: small scale only; no contradiction check unless you add one. A personal starting point.

Close cousin of the wiki: the wiki adds the schema, ingest and lint, and provenance discipline. Early rung versus later rung.

1. How big, how fast changing?Small and stable favors long context; large and changing favors RAG or a wiki.
2. What do people ask?Lookups favor RAG; synthesis, comparison and "where do sources disagree" favor a wiki or graph.
3. Who will maintain it?RAG needs someone to manage the library and pipeline; a wiki needs someone to review what the model writes. With no owner, any of them decays.

Scenario: the nonprofit director and 80 documents

Researching local housing programs across five counties over three months: guidelines, news, board minutes, interview notes. Comparative questions, facts changing, a board briefing at the end.

Paste into long contextFine for 10 documents and one question; awkward here.
Plain RAGGood for "What does County B say about income limits?" Weak for "where do the five counties' rules conflict?"
Compiled wikiPage per county, program and source, plus a page of conflicts; originals untouched.
Her routineReviews summaries of the most important sources, a contradiction and stale check every couple of weeks, one rule: no claim without a link to its source.
The load-bearing checkA wrong summary could mislead later pages, so for the three documents behind her board recommendations she reads the originals herself.

Failure modes of compiled knowledge, and a checklist

Compounding errorsA mistaken summary becomes the basis for later pages. Fix: immutable sources, citations, spot checks, lint.
Silent driftPages get rewritten and meaning shifts. Fix: version control; review changes in batches.
Over-trustIt looks organized and authoritative. Organization is not accuracy.
Scale limitsThe index works at moderate scale; beyond it, search or a combination.
Privacy and accessAn agent needs file access: decide what it can see, only read, and change. A creator's approach, not an audited design.
Maintenance costWithout a human curator it tends to degrade.
  1. What is the raw source of truth, and is it protected from edits?
  2. Is there a rule to cite sources on every claim?
  3. Who reviews what the model writes, how often and how?
  4. How will we notice contradictions and stale pages?
  5. What is the plan when the collection outgrows the index?

Scenario 2: a five-person product team

Decisions made in meetings and chat; six months later nobody remembers why.

Sources: meeting notes and key chat threads, untouched
→
After each meeting: model drafts decision pages (date, who, options, what and why) and updates links
→
A person reviews in a minute or two and approves
→
Monthly lint: conflicting decisions, stale open questions
What it buysFaster onboarding, fewer repeated debates, a clear record of reasoning
RisksRecording a decision that was only discussed, or attributing a view to the wrong person; sensitive content in notes. Human review at ingest; raw notes stay the source of truth; decide who can see the wiki.
A one-afternoon start (hours 1 and 2)Hour 1: pick a topic, gather ten sources into a "sources" folder, do not edit them. Hour 2: write a one-page instruction file: what the wiki is for, page types, every claim links to a source, conflicts marked rather than smoothed over.

Hours 3 and 4, and four myths

  1. Hour 3. Ask your AI tool to read the first three sources and create the pages. Read, correct, add the lessons to the instruction file.
  2. Hour 4. Add the rest one at a time, reviewing. Ask five real questions; check use of the wiki and citations. Run a contradiction and gap check.
Myth 1: no hallucinationThe model writes the wiki, so it can write mistakes in. Protection is sources, citation and review.
Myth 2: a particular appPlain text files, links and a maintenance routine. The tool matters less than the discipline.
Myth 3: it replaces search or RAGIndex may suffice at moderate scale; at larger scale combine a wiki for synthesis with search for details.
Myth 4: more automation is betterAutomate drafting, keep human approval; loosen only as you measure how often drafts are right.

Six questions before you build

  1. Is my material small and stable enough to benefit? Moderate pile, topic you return to. If huge or changing by the minute, search over raw sources, maybe alongside a wiki for key concepts.
  2. Who will review? Otherwise: a confident pile of unchecked summaries.
  3. What is sensitive? Anything you feed the model may leave your machine, depending on tool and terms. Keep identifiers, credentials, confidential material out unless terms and access are checked.
  4. How will I know it is getting better? A list of real questions; note whether answers were useful and correct.
  5. What is my fallback? Go back to the raw sources, which stay untouched with every claim linked.
  6. What would make me stop? If maintenance costs more than it saves after a month or two, simplify or stop.

One more pass

Raw sources stay untouched
→
A model writes and maintains linked pages, following your instruction file
→
Questions answered mostly from the pages

Front-loads the work: you pay when sources arrive and get faster, more consistent answers later. Cost: maintenance and a new risk, since a wrong summary now sits in your pages and gets repeated. Reference & cheat sheet: Compiled knowledge and the LLM wiki

Companion to episode 5 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

Instead of searching raw documents on every question, have an AI read sources once and maintain a linked set of notes between you and the raw material. Karpathy's public note describes three layers (raw sources that are never edited, a model-written wiki, and a schema file of rules), three operations (ingest, query, lint) and two helper files (an index and a log). This is automated "combination" from SECI (companion 3), with a human steering.

Head-to-head: four approaches, five dimensions

Long context RAG Compiled wiki Simple second brain
Setup effort Almost none Moderate to high Moderate (schema, workflow, review) Very low
Whole-collection questions Possible if it fits Weak Potentially strong (synthesis exists already) Limited
Traceability Ask for quotes Strong if built to cite Strong if every claim links to sources Simple (one topic per file)
Freshness You paste the latest Re-index changes Needs ingest and lint Entirely on the human
Main risk Cost; middle problem Misses; context-free chunks Compounding errors; over-trust Small scale only

Choose by three questions: how big and how fast-changing is the collection, what will people ask, and who will maintain it.

Where compiled knowledge fails

Compounding errors, silent drift, over-trust (organized is not accurate), scale limits (Karpathy's note says the index works at moderate scale), privacy and access (what may the agent see, only read, or change), and maintenance cost (a wiki without a human curator tends to degrade).

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A nonprofit director collects about eighty documents about housing programs in five counties over three months. Questions are comparative: "Where do the counties' rules conflict?" Plain RAG answers county-by-county look-ups but struggles with that. A compiled wiki gives a page per county, program and source, plus a conflicts page, updated as documents arrive. Originals stay untouched. For the three documents behind board recommendations she reads the originals herself. That is the load-bearing check.

Generalist decision card: five questions before building one

  1. What is the raw source of truth, and is it protected from edits?
  2. What is the rule for citing sources on every claim?
  3. Who reviews what the model writes, how often and how?
  4. How will we notice contradictions and stale pages?
  5. What is the plan when the collection outgrows the index?

Counterpoints and limits

  • "Compiled beats retrieval" is a claim without a controlled comparison so far. Treat it as a promising pattern.
  • Karpathy's date was not verified and is not stated here.
  • The Perplexity argument is paraphrased from the post's introduction. Read its validation section before trusting any figure.
  • The access-control ideas relayed from Wanderloots are a tutorial's design, not a security audit.

Try it (one afternoon)

Pick 10 sources you can share with an AI tool. Keep them in a read-only folder. Write a one-page rule file (structure, citation rule, "never edit sources"). Have the model build a summary page per source and an index. Spot-check three pages against the originals. Keep it under version control so you can see changes.
