Companion 2: How models work and fail

Companion to episode 2 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

Four ideas explain most behavior: tokens, next-token prediction, a fixed snapshot of knowledge, and a context window. Five failures follow predictably: confident invention, a forgotten middle, a stale snapshot, instability, and a drifting conversation. Each has a cheap response. The skill that ties them together is context engineering: deciding what goes on the desk.

Key terms

Term Plain meaning
Token A chunk of text. Limits and prices are usually counted in tokens, not words.
Context window The maximum bundle of text the model can use at once.
Compaction Summarizing a long conversation and starting a new window with the summary. Risk: dropping details whose importance appears later.
Context engineering Designing everything on the desk: instructions, documents, tool results, history, memory. Wider than prompt wording.

The five failures and the one-line fix

Failure What you see Cheap response
Confident invention Specific, fluent, wrong quotes or citations, worst on rare, recent or private facts Ask for sources, open them
Forgotten middle Fact buried in a long paste is missed Put key material first or last; trim the rest
Stale snapshot Outdated answers with no warning Turn search on; compare
Instability Different answers to the same question Ask more than once; trust what stays constant
Drifting conversation Early instructions fade Start fresh with a short summary

Head-to-head: three answers to "models fail, so what?"

Wait for a bigger model Use a longer window Give better inputs
Helps most with General knowledge, reasoning quality Small, stable material, fast start Large or changing material, traceable answers
Does not fix Your private facts that no public model has seen Cost, and the middle problem Weak reasoning in the model itself
Effort None Low Moderate and ongoing
Fits when your main risk is Reasoning quality Convenience Missing knowledge

These are not strict rivals. Organizations use all three. The choice is about where your main risk lies.

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A manager pastes a 60-page report and asks for the one deadline that changed. The assistant misses it, because it sits mid-document. She moves the relevant section to the top with a two-line brief and tells the model to say so if the answer is absent. The answer is right and quotable. Lesson: the model was not stupid; the desk was messy.

Generalist decision card

  1. What does the model need to know that it cannot already know?
  2. Is that on the desk in clean form?
  3. What should it do if the answer is absent?
  4. Which one claim will I verify?

Counterpoints and limits

  • The "Lost in the Middle" findings come from models tested in 2023. Newer models may behave differently. Re-test with your own material.
  • Statements about randomness settings in chat products are general knowledge and vary by product.
  • Context engineering can be a rebrand of prompt engineering. Ask what changed in practice.

Try it (5 minutes)

Hide one odd fact in the middle of a long article and ask about it. Move it to the start and ask again. Compare.

Quiz

  1. What are limits and prices usually counted in?
  2. Why can a bigger window still miss a fact?
  3. Name two of the five failures and their fixes.
  4. Prompt engineering vs context engineering: what is the difference?
  5. When is a longer window the simplest good choice?
Show answers

1. Tokens. 2. Models often use the start and end of long inputs better than the middle. 3. See table. 4. Wording the request vs everything on the desk. 5. One contract or one report, small and stable.

Maturity thread

Oracle use (ask, accept) to desk-aware use to repeatable briefs to systems that supply context automatically.
