The chatbot was doing what it was built to do: produce text that fits. The episode promises five ideas (token, context window, forgetting, varying answers, confident mistakes), then one skill that ties them together: context engineering.

Thread 1: knowledge managementGetting the right information to the right place
Thread 2: maturity modelsHow a practice grows from improvised to dependable

→
2. Five failure modes, each with a quick test
→
3. Bigger model, longer window, or better inputs?
→
4. What to do Monday morning

Ideas 1 to 3: tokens, next-token prediction, the snapshot

1. TokensChunks of text. Limits, prices and speed are counted in tokens, not words.
2. Predict the next tokenRead everything so far, predict the next token, add it, repeat. No separate fact-check step.
3. A snapshotTraining ends. Knowledge stops changing.

Producing a fact and producing a plausible-sounding sentence are the same kind of act. Token counts per word vary by model and language.

Cutoff, the context window, and why it seems to remember

Knowledge cutoffNew information from search or files arrives as text in the conversation, not as new knowledge inside the model.
Context windowHidden product instructions, the conversation so far, attached documents, search results, your newest question. The maximum size is counted in tokens.
Apparent memoryWhen a chatbot seems to remember you from last week, the product stored notes and put them on the desk. The model starts fresh every time.

A bigger desk does not mean everything on it is used equally well.

Maturity: from oracle to reader

Next: a repeatable way of supplying material (later episodes)
Understands the desk: the answer depends on what is in front of the model, and starts to control it
Oracle: ask, accept, move on

The shift from treating the model as an oracle to treating it as a reader of what you give it is called the single biggest step in everyday AI skill.

Failure 1: confident invention

HallucinationSpecific, fluent, wrong: a quote, citation, statistic or legal case. A plausible fake looks a lot like a real fact to the model.
Worst onThings that are rare, recent, private or highly specific
Usually fine onA well-known historical date

Example from the episode: the exact wording of a clause in your contract is not something it can know. Researchers have written surveys cataloging this behavior.

Test 1, then failure 2: the forgotten middle

Test 1Ask for three sources on a narrow topic you know well, with links. Check each exists.
Failure 2: the forgotten middlePaste a long document and ask about something in the middle: it may be missed. The "Lost in the Middle" paper found performance was often best at the start or end and worse in the middle, even for models built for long inputs. Models have changed since, so test yours.
Test 2Hide one odd fact in the middle of a long article, ask; then move it to the start and ask again.

Failure 3: the stale snapshot

Stale snapshotAsk about something recent without search and it may answer from outdated knowledge, without saying so. The model is often poor at knowing where its own knowledge ends.
Test 3Ask about a recent event in a field you follow, once with web search off and once with it on. Compare.

Failure 4: instability

Different answers to the same questionUsually by design: the model does not always pick the single most likely token. Some tools let you turn the randomness down.
What to doNever treat one chat answer as a measurement. Run it more than once, or ask for reasoning and sources. The stable parts across runs are more trustworthy.
Test 4Ask for a short summary of the same paragraph three times in separate chats. Notice what stays constant.

Failure 5: the drifting conversation, and compaction

Early instruction: keep answers short
→
10 messages later: long again
→
Or builds on an earlier mistake
FixIn a long chat, start a new one and paste a short summary. Some products do this automatically (compaction). The risk, as the vendor itself notes: too aggressive and subtle details drop out.

Five failures: none is stupidity

Confident invention
Forgotten middle
Stale snapshot
Unstable answers
Cheap responsesPut the right material on the desk, keep the desk tidy, check the one claim that matters, and ask more than once.

Three competing answers

1. Wait for a bigger modelPartly true, but your private contracts and history are in no public model.Good for: general knowledge
2. A longer windowNo engineering needed, but every token costs money and time, and more material can bury the fact. Vendor guidance warns of context pollution.Good for: a small, stable set of material
3. Better inputsThe right documents, in the right form, at the right time, with clear instructions. This is context engineering.Good for: large, changing material and traceable answers

Check your employer's rules and the tool's data terms before pasting material in.

They are not strict rivals

Worried about missing knowledgeBetter inputs
Worried about reasoning qualityA stronger model
Worried about convenienceA longer window

Organizations use all three. The decision is where your main risk lies.

Context engineering and the four-step habit

Prompt engineeringHow you word the request
Context engineeringEverything on the desk: instructions, documents, tool results, history, memory
  1. Decide what the model needs that it cannot already know: your situation, constraints, sources.
  2. Put it on the desk in clean form. A short clear brief beats a long messy dump.
  3. Tell it what to do when the answer is not there ("say so"). Improves odds, no guarantee.
  4. Check the one claim your decision depends on against the source.
Beyond: a system supplies the context automatically
A repeatable brief and a habit of checking
Pasting things into a chat ad hoc

Context engineering is knowledge management

Someone has to decideWhat knowledge exists, where it lives, whether it is current, and how it gets to the point of use. That job existed in libraries, consulting firms and hospitals long before AI. Next episode looks at what that discipline learned.

Scenarios 1 and 2: the contract and the tax deadline

Oracle: contractPaste 40 pages, ask for key risks. A smooth summary may skip a clause on page 26 and not say so.
Reader: contractAsk for every passage on termination, notice and early-exit fees, with quotes and section numbers, list what is unsure, check two or three quotes.
Oracle: tax form deadlineAnswers from its snapshot; the deadline may have changed or the form may be confused with another.
Reader: tax form deadlineTurn on search or paste the official instructions, answer only from that page and give the link, then open it and read the one line that matters.

Scenario 3 and the pattern: source versus reader

Oracle: the statisticModels are agreeable, so a request that implies the answer you want makes a fitting number more likely.
Reader: the statisticAsk where such a statistic might be found and go there, or supply the source and ask for the number and the exact sentence.
The patternWhen the model is the source, you are exposed. When the model is the reader of a source you can check, you are protected.

Common questions: your chats, citations, different models

Is the AI learning from my chats?Depends on product and settings. Check privacy settings before pasting anything confidential; business versions have different terms.
If it cites a source, can I trust it?Not automatically. A citation is a starting point for checking. Linked, retrieved documents are safer, but read the passage.
Why do models differ?Different data, training, instructions, sampling. Disagreement is a flag; agreement is not proof.

Fourth question: prompt tricks

Politeness, "deep breath", rolesSome help in some cases, and effects change by model and version. Better investment: clear instructions, supply the material, ask for evidence, check. Those habits do not expire.

Five experiments for this week

  1. Sampling. A creative question vs a factual one, three times each. Variety is a feature in one and a warning in the other.
  2. The long paste. Hide "the meeting room code is 4417" in the middle, then at the start. Results depend on model and length; build your own sense.
  3. The changing world. Ask about a recent product version, then its cutoff and how it knows. Compare with the product website.
  4. The permission to doubt. Ask a hard factual question normally, then add "if you are not sure, say so and list what you would need to check."

Experiment 5: the explanation test

Explain a concept you know well, then one you do notYou catch mistakes more easily in the first case. That gap is the risk: where you need AI most is often where you can check it least.

The episode in six sentences

  1. A model reads tokens and predicts what comes next.
  2. It only works with what is on its desk, which is limited and used unevenly.
  3. It has a cutoff and no memory unless the product adds one.
  4. It fails in five predictable ways, so you can design around them.
  5. Best defense: supply real material, ask for quotes, check the one claim that matters.
  6. The question is not "is the model smart enough" but "can I verify this?"

Recap, the maturity rung, homework

Where this sits on the ladderThe shift from oracle to reader. If you have started choosing what goes on the desk, you have moved up a rung.
HomeworkRun two tests: the invented source and the moved fact. Write down what you saw. Personal evidence beats anything the episode can tell you.

Reference & cheat sheet: How models work and fail

Companion to episode 2 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

Four ideas explain most behavior: tokens, next-token prediction, a fixed snapshot of knowledge, and a context window. Five failures follow predictably: confident invention, a forgotten middle, a stale snapshot, instability, and a drifting conversation. Each has a cheap response. The skill that ties them together is context engineering: deciding what goes on the desk.

Key terms

Term Plain meaning
Token A chunk of text. Limits and prices are usually counted in tokens, not words.
Context window The maximum bundle of text the model can use at once.
Compaction Summarizing a long conversation and starting a new window with the summary. Risk: dropping details whose importance appears later.
Context engineering Designing everything on the desk: instructions, documents, tool results, history, memory. Wider than prompt wording.

The five failures and the one-line fix

Failure What you see Cheap response
Confident invention Specific, fluent, wrong quotes or citations, worst on rare, recent or private facts Ask for sources, open them
Forgotten middle Fact buried in a long paste is missed Put key material first or last; trim the rest
Stale snapshot Outdated answers with no warning Turn search on; compare
Instability Different answers to the same question Ask more than once; trust what stays constant
Drifting conversation Early instructions fade Start fresh with a short summary

Head-to-head: three answers to "models fail, so what?"

Wait for a bigger model Use a longer window Give better inputs
Helps most with General knowledge, reasoning quality Small, stable material, fast start Large or changing material, traceable answers
Does not fix Your private facts that no public model has seen Cost, and the middle problem Weak reasoning in the model itself
Effort None Low Moderate and ongoing
Fits when your main risk is Reasoning quality Convenience Missing knowledge

These are not strict rivals. Organizations use all three. The choice is about where your main risk lies.

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A manager pastes a 60-page report and asks for the one deadline that changed. The assistant misses it, because it sits mid-document. She moves the relevant section to the top with a two-line brief and tells the model to say so if the answer is absent. The answer is right and quotable. Lesson: the model was not stupid; the desk was messy.

Generalist decision card

  1. What does the model need to know that it cannot already know?
  2. Is that on the desk in clean form?
  3. What should it do if the answer is absent?
  4. Which one claim will I verify?

Counterpoints and limits

  • The "Lost in the Middle" findings come from models tested in 2023. Newer models may behave differently. Re-test with your own material.
  • Statements about randomness settings in chat products are general knowledge and vary by product.
  • Context engineering can be a rebrand of prompt engineering. Ask what changed in practice.

Try it (5 minutes)

Hide one odd fact in the middle of a long article and ask about it. Move it to the start and ask again. Compare.

Quiz

  1. What are limits and prices usually counted in?
  2. Why can a bigger window still miss a fact?
  3. Name two of the five failures and their fixes.
  4. Prompt engineering vs context engineering: what is the difference?
  5. When is a longer window the simplest good choice?
Show answers

1. Tokens. 2. Models often use the start and end of long inputs better than the middle. 3. See table. 4. Wording the request vs everything on the desk. 5. One contract or one report, small and stable.

Maturity thread

Oracle use (ask, accept) to desk-aware use to repeatable briefs to systems that supply context automatically.
