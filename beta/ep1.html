Why it sounds right and is wrong, and the desk

Usually reliable onCommon facts, widely used code patterns, standard explanations
Least reliable onRare, recent, private or very specific things: a minor court case, your refund policy, last week's news, a page number
THE DESK (context window)
Your question · any documents you paste in · the conversation so far
Plus what it absorbed in training, up to its knowledge cutoff

Name for the failure: hallucination, fluent and confident text that is wrong or made up. Desks have grown but are not unlimited, and information buried in the middle of a long input can be handled less reliably.

No memory, and the desk rule

No memory of its ownChat products that "remember" usually save notes and put them back on the desk. Products differ, so check yours.
Remember the deskEverything good and bad about working with these models comes down to what is on the desk when the model answers.

Three kinds of AI: predictive, generative, agentic

PredictivePredicts a number or category from dataDo we have good data, and can we measure whether it works?
GenerativeProduces new text, images, code, audioWhere can a fluent first draft help, and who checks it?
AgenticA generative model connected to tools, able to take stepsWhat is it allowed to do, and who is accountable when it acts?

A wrong chatbot gives a bad answer. A wrong agent can do a bad thing.

Back to the desk: three ways to get knowledge onto it

The real questionNot "which model is smartest" but how to get the right knowledge onto the desk at the right moment. That is a knowledge management problem, and it existed before AI.
1. Retrieval (RAG)Keep documents in a searchable store; find the relevant pieces and put them on the desk with the question
2. Compiled knowledgeHave the AI read sources once and build a structured, linked set of notes, kept up to date
3. Structured connectionsKnowledge graphs record how things relate. GraphRAG builds one from documents to answer broad questions across a collection
The split, in every episodeMachine does the grunt work; the human owns the judgment (what counts as a source, what is trusted, what is out of date).
Mini decision: 200 documents, one AIAsk first: (1) who maintains these and how will we know they are out of date? (2) are the questions look-ups in one document or big-picture across all? (3) when the answer is wrong, who notices?

Three experiments you can run in ten minutes

  1. The invented detail. Ask about something small and specific that is not famous. Notice the confident tone: tone is not evidence.
  2. The same question twice, in separate conversations. Different answers show the prediction machinery. Agreement does not make an answer correct.
  3. The document on the desk (starts at 19:38). Use a document you are allowed to share; ask with nothing attached, then attached with "answer only from this document, quote the sentence, say so if it is not there". Compare.

Four common misunderstandings

"It looked it up."Only if search or document access is on. Ask what sources it used.
"It remembers me."Find out how: a stored note you can read and delete, or just recent chat?
"Confident means better."Fluency tells little about correctness. Can it cite something you can open?
"Newer and bigger is always better."Look at the desk before you shop for a new model.

Not an oracle and not a toy: a fast, fluent assistant that needs good material and a careful reader.

Three habits, and the recap

1. Right material on the deskAttach the document and ask it to answer only from that. Reduces made-up answers, does not eliminate them.
2. Ask for evidence, check the one thing that mattersAudit the load-bearing claim.
3. Match autonomy to stakesDraft freely; act only where mistakes are cheap and reversible; human yes before anything that sends, spends, deletes or speaks for you.
