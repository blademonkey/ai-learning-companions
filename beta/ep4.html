What RAG is, and steps 1 to 3

Retrieval-Augmented Generation. From a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues: a language model combined with a separate searchable collection of documents. In desk terms: automatically fetch the right pages and put them on the desk before the model answers.

1. PrepareSplit documents into chunks, a few hundred tokens each
→
2. IndexEach chunk becomes an embedding (a fingerprint of meaning), stored in a vector database
→
3. RetrieveThe question gets the same treatment; take the closest few chunks

Step 4, and why people like it

4. GenerateThe prompt = question + retrieved chunks + instructions ("answer only from this; say if it is not there"). Ideally the answer points to the chunks used.
Private and currentUpdate the index when a document changes; no retraining
TraceableAnswers can point back to sources
Ordinary modelsWorks with models you can already use
In knowledge management termsRAG is a retrieval system. It does not create or organize knowledge; it finds pieces of it. That explains both the strengths and the weaknesses.

Weak points 1 to 3

1. Not in the libraryMissing, or a scanned image the system could not read. Back to episode 3: tacit gap, stale, conflicting.
2. Chunking cuts the meaning"The penalty is 5%": which penalty, which client, which agreement? Exactly the accounting failure.
3. Similar is not relevantClose in meaning is not the passage that answers. Embeddings can be poor at names, codes and numbers, so many systems add keyword search.

Weak point 4: the wrong number of pieces

Too fewYou may miss the answer
Too manyYou flood the desk and bring back "lost in the middle" from episode 2

Weak points 5 and 6

5. The model misuses what it hasIt can overlook the right chunk, blend it with memory, or sound more confident than the text supports. "Answer only from the material" helps, no guarantee.
6. Questions about the whole library"Main themes across all engagements?" "Where do our policies contradict?" RAG fetches a handful of chunks. Microsoft Research (GraphRAG paper): such global questions are really summarization tasks. Returns in episode 6.

The pattern

Most failures are not the language modelThey are the library, the chunking, the search and the question: things a generalist can reason about without a technical background.

Common improvements, as you will hear them in a pitch

Better chunkingCut at sections and headings; keep titles with their content
Add context to chunksAnthropic's contextual retrieval: attach where the chunk comes from, e.g. "from the Hendricks engagement letter, penalties section"
Hybrid search and re-rankingMeaning search plus keyword matching, then a second model reorders candidates

Anthropic reports 49% fewer failed retrievals with their method, and 67% combined with re-ranking. These are vendor-reported figures. All of these are knowledge management practices: structure, labels, ownership, testing.

Worked scenario: a sensible plan for the 800 documents

Type A: lookupsA specific client or matter, e.g. the Hendricks penalty
Type B: broad questions"Which engagement letters lack a liability cap?"
  1. Fix the library: scope, remove duplicates and superseded versions, name an owner (episode 3 work).
  2. Add metadata: client, document type, date, status; use it as a filter.
  3. Type A: retrieval with the improvements; require the assistant to cite passage and document name.
  4. Type B: not plain retrieval. A structured table (a row per letter, a column per item) or an approach built for whole-collection questions (next two episodes).
  5. Test: 30 real questions with known answers and sources; measure right document and right answer; repeat before launch and after every change.
  6. Review rule: for legal or financial consequences, a draft with citations that a person verifies.

Not a clever tool but a repeatable process with an owner, a test set and a review step: from improvised experiments to a defined, measured practice.

Four main ways to give a model your knowledge

1. Paste it inSmall material, asked about right away. Simple; costlier and less reliable as it grows.
2. RAGLarge collections, mostly lookups, answers that cite sources. Weaker for whole-collection questions.
3. Compiled knowledgeAn AI reads sources once and keeps an organized, linked set of notes (an "LLM-maintained wiki", described by Andrej Karpathy). Episode 5.
4. Knowledge graphsExplicit relationships between things; good for connections and broad themes. Episode 6.

Fine-tuning (further training on your material) is mentioned as a poor fit for changing facts: updating a fact means training again, while retrieval or a wiki just needs a document edit. None of these is the winner: collection size, question type, rate of change and need for traceability decide.

Four questions to ask anyone proposing RAG

  1. What is the library, who owns it, how does it stay current?
  2. What kinds of questions: lookups or whole-collection? If both, what is the plan for the second?
  3. How will we test it? Ask to see real questions and scores.
  4. What happens when it is wrong? Who sees the answer, and what check is required before action?

If a proposal cannot answer these, the technology is not the problem yet.

Tracing one wrong answer, step by step

  1. Was the right document in the library? Yes, indexed.
  2. Sensible chunks? No: cut at fixed lengths, so the penalty clause chunk lost the client name.
  3. What did search retrieve? Many late-filing chunks from many clients, since they all sound alike. The Hendricks chunk ranked in the top 10, not the top 3.
  4. How many pieces went to the model? Three, and not the Hendricks one.
  5. What did the model do? Answered from the chunk it had. It did its job.
Fix 1Add context to each chunk
Fix 2Filter by client before searching
Fix 3Retrieve a wider set, then re-rank

Cheap compared to a bigger model, which would not have helped because the right text never reached the desk.

Common questions

Do I need a vector database?Not always. For small collections keyword search or well-organized files with an index may do. Choose by size and question type, not fashion.
How many documents is too many?No fixed number. 100 organized beats 1,000 messy. If answers get worse as you add, suspect conflicts and duplicates first.
Spreadsheets, tables, scansTables lose structure as text; scans need text recognition, which can add errors. Test those cases. For tabular facts a database query or structured table is often more reliable.
Where does my data go?Depends on the setup. Ask exactly what leaves your environment, what the provider retains, under what terms. For regulated or confidential material, this comes before quality.
Can RAG stop hallucination?It can reduce it, not eliminate it. Citations, "say when it is not found", and a human check on important claims.
How do I know it is working?Track two things separately: did it retrieve the right passage, and was the final answer correct? That tells you whether to fix the library, the search or the instructions.

Five decisions, in order

  1. Question and audience. "Help staff find the right leave policy", not "make our documents smart".
  2. Scope. Start small: current, owned, not sensitive. Leave out old versions; resolve disagreements now.
  3. Splitting and labeling. Each piece carries document, section, date and who it applies to.
  4. How you check. 20 real questions with answers before building, including some whose right answer is "not in the documents". Score finding and answering separately; repeat after every change.
  5. Rule for trust. When an answer can be used directly, when it needs a human check, when the system must say it does not know. Human check for money, health, legal rights or other people's data. Name an owner and a monthly failure review.

Model choice is not on the list: it comes after a clean library, sensible pieces and a test.

Five warning signs

1. Nobody can list the documentsIf you cannot list the sources, you cannot trust the answers.
2. Confident, no citationsA good system shows where each claim came from.
3. Demos but no test setThree hand-picked questions tell you very little.
4. The library never changesPolicies, prices and products change; answers will drift.
5. First reaction to failure: switch modelsSometimes helps; more often the cause is upstream.

Reference & cheat sheet: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

Companion to episode 4 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

RAG fetches the right pages from your documents and puts them on the model's desk before it answers. The 2020 paper by Lewis and colleagues combined a language model with a separate searchable collection. Most RAG failures are about the library, the chunking, the search and the question, not the language model. That makes them reasoning problems a generalist can follow.

The pipeline in four steps

  1. Prepare: split documents into chunks (a few paragraphs each).
  2. Index: turn each chunk into an embedding (a numeric fingerprint of its meaning) and store it in a vector database.
  3. Retrieve: fingerprint the question and take the closest chunks.
  4. Generate: the model answers from the question, the chunks and instructions such as "use only this material and say if the answer is missing".

Six weak points and what fixes them

Weak point Example Common fix
Knowledge not in the library Scanned image nobody could read Fix the library (see companion 3)
Chunking cuts meaning "The penalty is 5 percent." Which penalty? Chunk at natural boundaries; add context to chunks
Similar is not relevant Closest text is the wrong client's Metadata filters; hybrid search; reranking
Wrong number of pieces Too few miss it; too many flood the desk Tune, and test
Model ignores or misuses chunks Blends in its own memory Instructions plus checking
Questions about the whole library "Where do our policies contradict each other?" Compiled knowledge or graph approaches (companion 5, later episodes)

Head-to-head: ways to give a model your knowledge

Paste it in RAG Compiled knowledge Knowledge graph Fine-tuning
Best for A few documents Large collections, look-ups, cited answers Synthesis and comparisons over a growing set Questions about connections and themes Style and task skill
Weak at Large or changing sets Whole-collection questions Needs review of what the model writes Setup Facts that change (retrain to update)
Setup Almost none Moderate to high Moderate High High

Decided by: size of collection, type of question, how often things change, and how much traceability you need.

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

An accounting firm asks, "What is the penalty for late filing for Hendricks?" The search returns a chunk about a different client with a similar clause. Fixes in order: tag documents by client and filter to Hendricks first, add each chunk's origin to its text, then test with thirty real questions with known answers. The thirty-question test is an illustration, not a sourced benchmark.

Generalist decision card: four questions for anyone proposing RAG

  1. What is the library, who owns it and how does it stay current?
  2. Will people ask look-ups or whole-collection questions? If both, what is the plan for the second?
  3. How will it be tested? Ask to see the real questions and the scores.
  4. What happens when it is wrong? Who sees the answer and what check comes before action? Warning sign: a demo with no evaluation set.

Counterpoints and limits

  • Anthropic reports large reductions in failed retrievals from its Contextual Retrieval method (49% and 67% figures). These are the company's own reported results on specific datasets. Do not read them as a general guarantee.
  • Plain RAG is weak for whole-collection questions. GraphRAG (Edge et al.) was proposed for query-focused summarization over a whole corpus, and has its own setup cost.
  • Some practitioners argue that for moderate collections, structured notes read directly can replace embedding search. Karpathy's LLM Wiki note says this from his own experience, not from a controlled test.

Try it (no code)

List your documents, name an owner, write 20 real questions with known answers, and decide the chunk boundary (section headings). Test any tool against the 20 questions before trusting it.
