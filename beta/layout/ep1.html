Why it sounds right and is wrong, and the desk

Usually reliable onCommon facts, widely used code patterns, standard explanations
Least reliable onRare, recent, private or very specific things: a minor court case, your refund policy, last week's news, a page number
THE DESK (context window)
Your question · any documents you paste in · the conversation so far
Plus what it absorbed in training, up to its knowledge cutoff

Name for the failure: hallucination, fluent and confident text that is wrong or made up. Desks have grown but are not unlimited, and information buried in the middle of a long input can be handled less reliably.

No memory, and the desk rule

No memory of its ownChat products that "remember" usually save notes and put them back on the desk. Products differ, so check yours.
Remember the deskEverything good and bad about working with these models comes down to what is on the desk when the model answers.

Three kinds of AI: predictive, generative, agentic

PredictivePredicts a number or category from dataDo we have good data, and can we measure whether it works?
GenerativeProduces new text, images, code, audioWhere can a fluent first draft help, and who checks it?
AgenticA generative model connected to tools, able to take stepsWhat is it allowed to do, and who is accountable when it acts?

A wrong chatbot gives a bad answer. A wrong agent can do a bad thing.

Back to the desk: three ways to get knowledge onto it

The real questionNot "which model is smartest" but how to get the right knowledge onto the desk at the right moment. That is a knowledge management problem, and it existed before AI.
1. Retrieval (RAG)Keep documents in a searchable store; find the relevant pieces and put them on the desk with the question
2. Compiled knowledgeHave the AI read sources once and build a structured, linked set of notes, kept up to date
3. Structured connectionsKnowledge graphs record how things relate. GraphRAG builds one from documents to answer broad questions across a collection
The split, in every episodeMachine does the grunt work; the human owns the judgment (what counts as a source, what is trusted, what is out of date).
Mini decision: 200 documents, one AIAsk first: (1) who maintains these and how will we know they are out of date? (2) are the questions look-ups in one document or big-picture across all? (3) when the answer is wrong, who notices?

Three experiments you can run in ten minutes

  1. The invented detail. Ask about something small and specific that is not famous. Notice the confident tone: tone is not evidence.
  2. The same question twice, in separate conversations. Different answers show the prediction machinery. Agreement does not make an answer correct.
  3. The document on the desk (starts at 19:38). Use a document you are allowed to share; ask with nothing attached, then attached with "answer only from this document, quote the sentence, say so if it is not there". Compare.

Four common misunderstandings

"It looked it up."Only if search or document access is on. Ask what sources it used.
"It remembers me."Find out how: a stored note you can read and delete, or just recent chat?
"Confident means better."Fluency tells little about correctness. Can it cite something you can open?
"Newer and bigger is always better."Look at the desk before you shop for a new model.

Not an oracle and not a toy: a fast, fluent assistant that needs good material and a careful reader.

Three habits, and the recap

1. Right material on the deskAttach the document and ask it to answer only from that. Reduces made-up answers, does not eliminate them.
2. Ask for evidence, check the one thing that mattersAudit the load-bearing claim.
3. Match autonomy to stakesDraft freely; act only where mistakes are cheap and reversible; human yes before anything that sends, spends, deletes or speaks for you.

Reference & cheat sheet: What AI actually is

Companion to episode 1 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

A language model predicts likely text. That makes it fluent and useful, and it also means it can state wrong things in a confident tone. It works only from what is on its "desk" (the text in front of it). So the practical skill is not finding a smarter model. It is putting the right material on the desk and checking the one claim that matters.

Key terms

Term Plain meaning
AI A label for many methods, coined in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal. The field has cycled between big promises and sober reality.
Language model A system trained to predict the next piece of text. It produces answers by repeating that step.
Desk Everything the model can see right now: your question, attached files, search results, hidden product instructions.
Knowledge cutoff The date training stopped. Later events are unknown unless the product supplies them as text.
Hallucination Fluent, specific, wrong output. Surveyed in the research literature.
Predictive, generative, agentic AI Predicts a number or label; produces new content; takes actions toward a goal. Each needs a different question: how accurate, how checkable, how reversible.

Head-to-head: three approaches to building AI

Rules written by people Learning from examples Deep learning (large models)
Strength Readable, predictable Handles messy data Handles language, images and more at scale
Weakness Brittle outside the rules Needs good examples Hard to inspect; can be confidently wrong
Ask Who wrote the rules and when? What examples, and are they like mine? What did you check, and what is my fallback?

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A team lead asks a chatbot, "What is our refund policy for annual plans?" with nothing attached. The answer sounds precise. It is a pattern of typical refund policies, not theirs. She pastes the actual policy and asks it to answer only from that text, quote the sentence used, and say so if the answer is missing. The second answer is shorter, quotable and checkable. She checks the one number a customer will rely on.

Generalist decision card

  • Before relying on an answer, ask: is the model working from my material or from general patterns?
  • Let AI draft freely. Let it act only where mistakes are cheap and reversible. Require a human yes before anything that sends, spends, deletes or speaks for you.
  • Do not share confidential documents with an AI tool without checking your employer's rules and the tool's data terms.

Counterpoints and limits

  • "Prediction" is a simple description of a complex system. Researchers debate how much understanding, if any, sits behind the fluency. The episode keeps to what is observable.
  • Agreement between two answers is not correctness. A model can repeat the same mistake.
  • The three-way split (predictive, generative, agentic) is a useful sorting, not a standard taxonomy.

Try it (10 minutes)

  1. Ask about a small, specific, non-famous detail. Note how confident the tone is.
  2. Ask the same open question twice in separate chats. Note what differs.
  3. Ask a question about a non-sensitive document, once without it and once attached with "answer only from this document."

Quiz (answers below)

  1. Why can a fluent answer still be wrong?
  2. What is the "desk"?
  3. When a chatbot seems to remember you, what is usually happening?
  4. Name one action AI should not take without a human yes.
  5. Where on a maturity ladder is "ask a chatbot and hope"?
Show answers

1. It is trained to produce likely text, not checked facts. 2. The text the model can see right now. 3. The product stores notes and places them on the desk; the model itself starts fresh. 4. Sending, spending, deleting or speaking for you. 5. The improvised rung.

Maturity thread

Improvised use (ask and accept) to repeatable use (a standard way to supply material and check output). The next rung is a method, not a bigger model.
