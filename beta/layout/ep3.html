Knowledge management, and tacit versus explicit

Explicit knowledgeCan be written down: a procedure, a specification, a price list, a formula
Tacit knowledgeLives in practice and experience: reading a room, hearing that a machine sounds wrong, bed six

Polanyi, 1966: "We can know more than we can tell." Nonaka (1994, Organization Science) argued organizations create knowledge by moving it between the two forms; explicit knowledge is the tip of the iceberg. Text on the desk is explicit knowledge, so everything tacit is invisible to an AI unless someone converts it. Quality of an AI system on your knowledge is capped by how much is explicit and how good that written form is.

SECI: socialization, externalization, combination

Running example: a home-renovation company and a veteran estimator.

SocializationTacit to tacit. The apprentice rides along and absorbs his instincts. Works, slow, does not scale.
ExternalizationTacit to explicit. Write the checklist, warning signs, questions. Hardest step; uses stories and examples.
CombinationExplicit to explicit. Checklist, past quotes, supplier lists become an estimating guide and template. AI is genuinely strong here.

Internalization, the spiral, and where AI helps

InternalizationExplicit to tacit. A new estimator uses the guide on 30 jobs until the habits live in her. The cycle restarts at a higher level: a spiral.
CombinationStrongest. Merge, summarize, reorganize, compare.
ExternalizationHelps with a person in the loop: interviews the expert, drafts, the expert corrects.
InternalizationIndirect: tutor or coach. Learning still happens in the person.
SocializationVery little. A model cannot ride along to the job site.

A fair critique of SECI

Conceptual framework, not an experimentally proven lawResearchers debate how cleanly knowledge divides into tacit and explicit, and whether the four-part cycle matches real organizations. A useful way to think, not a measurement instrument.

Worked example: why the internal assistant stalls

A mid-sized company builds an assistant for policy questions. The vendor demo is impressive; six months later the project is stuck. Almost always it is one of five knowledge problems:

1. UnwrittenAnswers live in a few long-serving heads; documents describe the official process, not the real one.
2. ConflictingThree documents, three answers. A model will happily use any of them.
3. StaleThe policy changed, the document did not.
4. ScatteredEmail, chat, drives, tickets, personal folders. It cannot use what it cannot reach.
5. UnownedNo one accountable for accuracy, so no one fixes the source.
None is a technology problemThey existed before AI. AI changes the cost of ignoring them: a human notices an old document and asks a colleague; a model reads what it is given and writes fluently. Messy knowledge gets more dangerous, good knowledge more valuable. Often the best investment is cleaning up and owning your knowledge.

Maturity models and CMMI

A maturity model describes stages of capability. CMMI (CMMI Institute, for software and systems development) has levels one to five, plus a level zero, incomplete. Renovation-company version:

5 Optimizing: keeps improving the process from the data, keeps what works
4 Quantitatively managed: tracks quote accuracy against actual costs
3 Defined: one documented estimating process everyone uses, with training
2 Managed: project-level plans, a quote template, copies of past quotes
1 Initial: improvised; the veteran prices by gut feel; nobody can quote when he is out

Using the ladder as a lens, and its cautions

Level 1A pilot that lives in one enthusiast's chat history
Level 3-ishA shared prompt library with a standard way to check outputs
Climbing to 4Measuring how often AI drafts get corrected, and using it to improve
Caution 1CMMI is for software and system processes. Applying it to AI adoption is an analogy, not an official use.
Caution 2Do not chase the level instead of the result. Tie improvement to business objectives.
Caution 3A model describes common patterns, not a guaranteed path. The highest level is not right for everything.

The five-question audit

  1. Is it written down, and does it reflect how things really work? Ask frontline people to compare the document with reality.
  2. Are there conflicting versions? Search the topic and count the different answers.
  3. Is it current, and how would you know? Look for dates and owners.
  4. Can it be found? Is there a map of where knowledge lives?
  5. Who owns it? Name a person, not a team.

Score yourself honestly. Several "no" answers mean your real project is knowledge work, not an AI tool. Once knowledge is in shape, AI helps maintain it: finding contradictions, flagging stale pages, drafting updates for humans to review.

Objections 1 to 3

"We already have a wiki"Last time a wrong page was removed? Every page owned? Can a new hire find a basic answer without asking? Never, no, no means storage, not managed knowledge.
"People will not write things down"Capture as a byproduct of work; show the benefit. AI lowers the cost: a voice memo becomes a page the author corrects.
"AI can read all our email and chat"Raw communication is opinions, drafts, jokes, outdated plans, private data. Better: AI proposes summaries and decision records; a person approves the official page.

Objection 4, and a 30-day start

"If tacit knowledge cannot be written down, is it hopeless?"No. Capture decisions and the reasons behind them, exceptions, warning signs, stories of what went wrong. Pair people on real work. Goal: reduce the risk of critical knowledge in one head.
  1. Week 1. Pick one area where questions repeat; list the 20 most common questions.
  2. Week 2. Find the current answer and source for each; mark missing, conflicting or stale.
  3. Week 3. Fix the worst 10: short plain pages, date and owner at the top, archive old versions.
  4. Week 4. Give a model just those pages, ask the 20 questions, compare, and trace each failure to a knowledge problem.

Result: a small owned, tested body of knowledge, a list of gaps, real evidence, and a first step up the ladder from improvised toward repeatable.

Seven questions for your workplace

  1. Where does the knowledge people need most live, and who can change it?
  2. What happens to it when a key person leaves, goes on leave or changes roles?
  3. For our most common questions, is there one agreed answer with a date and an owner?
  4. How would a new person find something that is not written down?
  5. What do people do when two documents disagree?
  6. Which material is sensitive, and who decided what an AI tool may read?
  7. How would we know if an AI answer based on our material were wrong?

Pick two; do not answer all in one meeting. None mentions a model or vendor, deliberately.

A word on the ladder

Language and a rough order of operationsIt can also encourage box ticking. Use levels as a conversation starter: where are we, what is the next sensible step, what result would it improve? If the answer is a label rather than a result, pause.

Reference & cheat sheet: Knowledge management before AI

Companion to episode 3 of the series. Read it before or after the episode. It repeats the ideas in a form you can scan, compare and test.

The idea in one paragraph

Most stalled AI projects stall on knowledge, not on technology. Knowledge management is the older discipline of deciding what is known, where it lives, whether it is current and who owns it. Nonaka's SECI model maps how knowledge moves between tacit (in people) and explicit (written). A maturity model such as CMMI gives a vocabulary for how repeatable a practice is.

Key terms

Term Plain meaning
Tacit knowledge What people know but cannot easily write down ("we can know more than we can tell", Polanyi, as quoted by Nonaka).
Explicit knowledge Written down: documents, checklists, data.
SECI Socialization (tacit to tacit), Externalization (tacit to explicit), Combination (explicit to explicit), Internalization (explicit to tacit).
Maturity model A description of stages of capability, with what each looks like.
CMMI levels Initial, Managed, Defined, Quantitatively Managed, Optimizing.

Where AI helps in SECI

Mode AI's role
Socialization Little. A model cannot ride along to the job site.
Externalization Helps if the expert stays in the loop: interviews, drafts, corrections.
Combination Strongest. Merging, summarizing, comparing explicit material.
Internalization Indirect, as tutor or coach. Learning still happens in the person.

The five knowledge problems (this series' own synthesis, from common patterns)

  1. Not written down. 2. Written in conflicting versions. 3. Stale. 4. Scattered. 5. Nobody owns it. None is a technology problem. AI raises the cost of ignoring them, because it does not apply a person's judgment about which document is old.

Head-to-head: where to invest first

Buy or build an AI tool Clean up and own the knowledge first
Fast to show Yes (demos) No
Fixes the five problems No Yes
Risk Confidently wrong answers from messy sources Slower visible progress
Fits when Knowledge is already in decent shape Answers to the audit below are mostly no

Worked scenario (invented, for illustration)

A renovation company's veteran estimator prices jobs by feel. Socialization: the apprentice rides along. Externalization: they write the checklist of warning signs. Combination: checklist, past quotes and price lists become an estimating guide. Internalization: a new estimator uses it on thirty jobs until it is habit. On the maturity ladder: improvised, then a quote template, then a documented process everyone uses, then measuring quote accuracy against actual costs, then improving the process from that data.

Generalist decision card: five-question audit before pointing AI at anything

  1. Is it written down and does it match how things really work?
  2. Are there conflicting versions?
  3. Is it current, and how would you know (dates, owners)?
  4. Can it be found (is there a map)?
  5. Who owns it (a person, not a team)? Several "no" answers mean the real project is knowledge work.

Counterpoints and limits

  • SECI is an influential conceptual framework, not an experimentally proven law. Researchers debate how cleanly knowledge divides into tacit and explicit.
  • CMMI was written for software and systems development processes. Using its level names for AI adoption is an analogy, not an official use.
  • Maturity ladders invite chasing the level instead of the result. CMMI Institute states that organizations benefit from a level when improvement focuses on business and performance results.
  • The five-problem list is a synthesis, not a sourced finding.

Try it (30 days, small)

Pick one body of knowledge you rely on. Run the audit. Name an owner. Fix the single worst problem. Re-run in 30 days.
